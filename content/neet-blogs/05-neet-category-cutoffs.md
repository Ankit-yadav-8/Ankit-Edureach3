# NEET Category-Wise Cutoffs Explained: General, OBC, SC, ST & EWS (2026)

Every year, a version of this message lands in our inbox:

*"Sir, my friend got a government seat with fewer marks than me. How is that possible? Did I do something wrong?"*

No, you didn't do anything wrong. And your friend didn't do anything shady. What you're running into is one of the most misunderstood parts of the entire NEET journey: **category-wise cutoffs.** Once you understand how they actually work, a lot of the confusion — and a lot of the resentment that gets stirred up online — quietly dissolves.

Let's break it down properly, without the heat that usually surrounds this topic.

## Two different things people mix up: qualifying cutoff vs admission cutoff

This is the confusion at the root of almost every category-cutoff question. There are *two* completely different "cutoffs" in NEET, and people constantly blur them.

**1. The qualifying cutoff (percentile).** This is the minimum bar to be considered "NEET-qualified" at all — expressed as a percentile. It differs by category: General sits at a higher percentile, while reserved categories (OBC/SC/ST) have a lower qualifying percentile. This just means you *cleared* NEET and are eligible to participate in counseling. Clearing it does **not** get you a seat by itself.

**2. The admission/closing cutoff.** This is the real one that decides seats — the marks/rank at which the *last seat* in a particular college, for a particular category and quota, got filled during counseling. This is what people actually mean when they ask "what's the cutoff for X college."

When your friend "got a seat with fewer marks," you're looking at difference number two: the admission cutoff for their category/quota was different from yours. That's the system working as designed, not a glitch.

## How reservation actually shapes the seat map

Here's the plain-language version. A pool of seats gets divided across categories — a portion reserved for OBC, SC, ST, EWS, and PwD candidates, with the rest as the open/General pool. Each category is essentially competing *within its own pool* for the reserved seats, in addition to General candidates competing for open seats.

The practical consequence: the closing cutoff for a reserved category at a given college is typically **lower** than the General closing cutoff at the *same* college. That's the entire mechanism behind "same college, different marks."

A crucial nuance people miss: **reserved-category candidates can also claim open/General seats** if their rank is high enough. So a strong reserved-category rank has access to *both* pools — which is why the highest reserved-category scorers land the very top colleges too.

## Category by category — what to keep in mind

We're deliberately not printing specific mark thresholds, because they move every single year with paper difficulty and competition, and a stale number does more harm than good. Instead, here's how to *think* about each:

### General / Unreserved (UR)
The highest qualifying bar and the highest admission cutoffs. If you're General, your planning is all about maximising reach within the open pool — which makes smart choice-filling especially important. See [how the counseling process works](/neet-counseling-process).

### OBC (Non-Creamy Layer)
A lower qualifying percentile than General, and access to OBC-reserved seats *plus* open seats if your rank reaches. The "non-creamy layer" certificate requirement is strict and format-sensitive — get it right well before counseling.

### SC / ST
The lowest qualifying percentiles, with dedicated reserved pools. This opens up genuine government-seat access at scores that wouldn't reach in the General pool. A valid, correctly-formatted caste certificate is non-negotiable at the reporting stage.

### EWS (Economically Weaker Section)
For General-category candidates meeting the economic criteria. Requires an EWS certificate in the exact prescribed format and validity window. Because the certificate rules are specific, this is one people commonly get tripped up on — sort it early.

### PwD (Persons with Disability)
A horizontal reservation cutting across categories, with its own qualifying provisions and a required disability certificate from an authorised centre. If applicable to you, understand the specific documentation early — it can't be arranged at the last minute.

## The certificate trap that costs reserved-category students their seats

Please read this part twice, because it's where we see genuine heartbreak.

Qualifying under a reserved category and *claiming* that seat are two different things. To claim it, you need the right certificate, in the **exact prescribed format**, valid for the **correct period**, issued by the **correct authority.** A certificate that's slightly wrong — wrong format, expired validity, wrong issuing authority — is treated by counseling authorities as if you have *no certificate at all.*

We've watched students who qualified comfortably in their category lose the reserved seat simply because the paperwork wasn't right. All that effort, undone by a form. Don't let it be you. Sort your category, domicile, and (if relevant) EWS/PwD certificates *now*, in the correct format, and keep multiple copies. We list the full document set in our [counseling process guide](/neet-counseling-process).

## Home-state quota × category = your real sweet spot

Here's the strategic insight that ties it together. Category cutoffs interact powerfully with **state quota.** Your best-value government seat often lives at the intersection of *your category* and *your home-state quota*, where the closing cutoff can be meaningfully more favourable than the All India Quota General cutoff for the same college.

## A calm word about the reservation debate

This topic gets heated online, and we're not here to litigate policy. What we'll say plainly: the system has rules, those rules apply to everyone in each category, and your job as an aspirant is to understand *your* position within them and plan the best possible seat from it. Energy spent resenting the framework is energy not spent building a smart choice list. Focus on what you control.

## How to actually use category cutoffs to plan

1. **Confirm your qualifying status** and which pools you can compete in (your category + potentially open seats).
2. **Get your certificates right early** — correct format, correct validity, correct authority.
3. **Predict with your real category and state**, not a generic chart. [Predict here](/neet-college-predictor).
4. **Target the intersection of category + home-state quota** for your best-value government options.
5. **Build a choice list that layers** category-reserved seats and open seats you can reach — never rely on just one pool.

## A final word from our desk

Category cutoffs aren't a mystery or a conspiracy — they're just a set of rules that create different paths to the same goal. The students who navigate them best are the ones who understand exactly which pools they can compete in, get their paperwork airtight, and plan accordingly.

Understand your position, protect your documents, and predict with your real details. That's how a category advantage — or any position — actually turns into a seat.
